# TCP Handshake: The Intercepted Packet

## Act 1: The Hook

Imagine you are a security analyst monitoring a network. You see a packet that looks suspicious. The payload is encrypted, but the header reveals something strange: a packet with just the SYN flag set has been intercepted, but it was sent to an IP address that doesn't exist on your network.

<Callout title="Socratic AI Prompt" type="info">
What does a single packet with only the SYN flag set typically represent? What happens if this packet reaches a non-existent destination?
</Callout>

## Act 2: Mental Model

To understand this, we need to break down the TCP three-way handshake. Think of it like a formal introduction between two people before they start a conversation.

1.  **SYN (Synchronize):** Person A says, "Hello, I would like to talk to you. I will start speaking from sequence number X."
2.  **SYN-ACK (Synchronize-Acknowledge):** Person B says, "Hello, I heard you. I acknowledge your sequence number X. I will also start speaking from sequence number Y."
3.  **ACK (Acknowledge):** Person A says, "I acknowledge your sequence number Y. Let us begin."

<Callout title="Socratic AI Prompt" type="info">
Why does TCP use a three-way handshake instead of just a two-way (request and response) connection establishment? What problem does the third step solve?
</Callout>

## Act 3: The Rabbit Hole

Returning to our intercepted packet. The SYN packet was sent, but the destination is a "black hole". If a server receives a SYN packet but never gets the final ACK, what happens to the connection?

<Callout title="Socratic AI Prompt" type="info">
If a malicious actor sends thousands of SYN packets to a server but never completes the handshake, what impact would this have on the server's resources? What is this attack called, and how can systems defend against it?
</Callout>
